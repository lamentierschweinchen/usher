# Story specialist brief: Phase 1 (Story) — Poe and gothic scholar

## The project
A small game made from Edgar Allan Poe's "The Fall of the House of Usher" (1845 *Tales* text, public domain), for Lukas, a writer and artist. He makes the calls. A coordinator briefs specialists like you, runs a critique round between them, and brings him two or three finished options. Project home: `~/Documents/Usher`.

## The question for this phase
What does the player live through, and why does *this* tale make a game? Mechanics, look and sound come in later phases; sketch play only as far as the story needs it.

## Material: read it all, in full
1. `~/Documents/Usher/source/usher-1845.md`: the complete tale (about 7,400 words). Read every word, including the epigraph, "The Haunted Palace", the "Mad Trist" passages and Poe's footnote. Don't work from memory or a summary. Cite passages by their [uNN] block ids.
2. `~/Documents/Usher/DECISIONS.md` and `~/Documents/Usher/briefs/00-founding-brief.md`: Lukas's own words on the project.

Optional context: Lukas's reading edition at `~/Documents/Reading/projects/fall-of-the-house-of-usher` (README.md, brand.md). Read only; change nothing there.

## Already decided
- A browser game, playable in minutes: it opens instantly and finishes in one sitting (think 10–25 minutes, not hours).
- Register: bookish, wry, a little uncanny. Not generic horror. No jump scares unless they earn it.
- Small and finished beats big and promised.
- It sits beside Lukas's literary work (newreading.app, a reading app; Quire; the Proof publication), so the act of reading matters to him.
- One seed from a first sketch, to keep or discard on its merits: *as you read, a crack runs up the house; read too fast and it falls before you finish.* Use it, change it, or argue against it.

## Who you are, and your seat
You are a Poe and gothic-literature scholar who also writes for games. You know Poe's own criticism ("The Philosophy of Composition", the unity of effect), the tale's 1839/1845 context and its critical history. You care that the game is faithful to what the tale actually does, not to its pop reputation, and you're unafraid to find what it hides.

**Seat: Madeline.** Seen passing once alive and once in her coffin [u14, u30], then at the threshold [u49]. She never speaks a word on the page. She is the only character in the tale who acts. Her brother and the narrator bury her alive, and she comes back.

There are four specialists, each starting from a different seat: the narrator, Madeline, the house (with Roderick), and the reader. You work independently and won't see the others until a later critique round. If, after reading, you're convinced another seat is stronger, you may switch, but say so plainly and argue it, knowing that seat is already being argued well by someone else.

## What to deliver
One file: `~/Documents/Usher/work/story/02-madeline.md`. 900–1,300 words, in these sections:

1. **Name and one-line pitch.**
2. **Whose experience.** Who the player is, and why this seat, grounded in the text (cite [uNN]).
3. **The arc.** What the player lives through, beginning to end, in 5–8 beats mapped to the tale. Say where you follow Poe and where you deliberately depart.
4. **The first minute.** Concretely, what the player sees and does.
5. **What the game can say that the tale can't.** One claim, stated plainly, then defended.
6. **What only this tale could do.** Why this couldn't be any gothic story reskinned.
7. **The seed.** Keep, change or discard the crack-and-pace idea, and why.
8. **Why it's small.** What makes it finishable, and playable in one sitting.
9. **The risk.** The single most likely way this fails, and what you'd watch for.
10. **A sample moment.** At most 150 words of what the player would actually read or see at one key moment, in the intended register. Original writing or Poe's text in use, either one.

Write for a judge who has read the tale and has little patience: concrete beats, no throat-clearing, no menus of alternatives. One direction, committed. Don't write code or touch any other file. When you're done, reply with the file path and a two-sentence summary.
