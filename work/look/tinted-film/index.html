Tinted Film

After Jean Epstein’s La Chute de la maison Usher (1928): a silver print dyed scene by scene, spoken in intertitles, joined by irises. Red is never a dye. It is printed over the top, and only where something really happened.

Three lines

The ideaPlay Epstein’s Usher as a tinted print: silver under grain, flicker and weave, each scene dyed by its mood (amber lamp, blue night, rose romance), spoken in Art Nouveau intertitles and joined by irises, with red printed over the dye where the truth is.

Why it fits UsherPoe’s house is failing light and unbearable sound, and silent film is where every sound must be printed and seen, never heard; it is also how this house was first filmed, so connoisseurs meet Epstein’s drapes and corridors, and everyone else gets a lovely old print.

The riskThe effects become the game: grain, flicker and weave must stay faint for small text, phones and sensitive eyes; and amber and rose sit close to red, so the truth must stay undyed, sharper and more saturated than anything the tint touches.
