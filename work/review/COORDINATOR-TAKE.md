# Coordinator's take (written before reading the experience critic)

*2026-10-27. Based on playing chapter I and the ending inside the spine, the builders' and art critic's reports, every chapter's screenshot, and the specs. I have not hand-played chapters II–VII at length.*

## What's genuinely good
- **The idea held.** Each chapter is a familiar game that tricks you in its own way: the spot-the-difference that cheats, the sliding puzzle played from the hole, the quest that turns over into Madeline's hands, the evidence that starts agreeing with you, the flight where looking back drowns the house. These are designs, not reskins.
- **The look.** Shadow Theatre is beautiful and reads as one hand. The tarn returning the house as light is the game's best image.
- **The writing** is the strongest single asset: short, dry, kind, Poe bent ("Is that wool? It's terribly loud."; "The family don't use the door, sir." / "Then how do they get out?").
- **The ending's account**, every score corrected in red, *say, rather*, is a real payoff when it lands.

## What's honestly weak
1. **Seven toys on a map, not yet one game.** The spine is a corridor: walk to the lit room, read three to five lines, play. The house has no secrets of its own, and nothing you do on the map matters. That's the gap between what we built (an anthology) and what Lukas asked for (a Zelda-like adventure with a plot).
2. **No question pulls you forward.** "Is she alive?" is answered by the reflection in the first minute. The best mystery we have, the Ushers burying a twin every generation, is only hinted at by paired portraits.
3. **Thin characters.** Roderick has about a dozen lines and the guest doesn't change. Without a relationship you've invested in, the confession lands as a clever joke rather than a sting.
4. **Invisible choices.** The confession depends on choices (spare the tap, leave a screw out, leave a noise unexplained) that most players won't know they made.
5. **Felt by bots, not hands.** Six of seven games were verified by scripted playthroughs. Difficulty, timing, frustration and fun are unmeasured, especially in the two puzzles (door, burial).
6. **Silence.** A silent film is right, but Roderick's guitar and "The Haunted Palace" ask for a small score we can make in code.
7. **Phones** get a small 3:2 stage and a "turn your phone" card.

## Verdict
A striking, witty, beautiful prototype: about 8/10 as concept and look, about 6/10 as a game you'd tell a friend to play. The second number is where the work is.

## Playtime
Estimated from the specs and builders' timings: letter and map about 4 min; tarn 1–1.5; door 2; studio 2.5; burial 1.5–3; nights 3; trist 2.5; flight 1. **About 18–20 minutes.**

**Ideal: 60–90 minutes**, one or two sittings. That's enough room for a house worth exploring, a mystery that develops, and characters you care about, while staying small and finished and short enough to replay for other endings. Roughly seven chapters of 8–10 minutes each, with exploration between them.

## The biggest moves for a full game (my ranking)
1. **Make the house the game.** An explorable cutaway whose rooms change each chapter, with things to inspect (paired portraits, Roderick's library [u27], his paintings [u17–u18]), each holding a fragment of the family secret.
2. **One mystery across the whole game:** the twin burials. Find who was buried in each generation, why the physician wants to see her, and what the letter didn't say. The player assembles it between chapters, a light Obra Dinn.
3. **Roderick as a companion.** Cheerfulness as a real relationship that your chapter choices raise or spend, with the confession reading it back.
4. **The mirror as the second act.** After the ending, the title's reflection, *A Burial of Some Weeks*, becomes a second playthrough from the tarn: the upside-down house, played as Madeline, with every chapter's trick turned inside out. It's what this tale's central image promises, and it doubles the game without doubling the art.
5. **Endings that hang on visible choices,** and a small procedural score (the speaking guitar, Von Weber's last waltz, the Haunted Palace).
