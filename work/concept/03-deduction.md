# The Direct Line

**Pitch:** Reconstruct the Usher family tree from the house and its reflection in the tarn, while every correct answer inks the fissure one zigzag further down the wall.

**Genre:** Deduction puzzle.

**The screen:** An ink facade on paper, mirrored in a black, rippling tarn. Click a window to see what it holds; its reflection holds something else [u26], and upside down, the buried vault floats at the top. Below, a parchment ledger with blanks you fill from a word bank of names and fates.

**The first minute:** The top window holds one boy; its reflection, two in a cradle. The register says "twins"; the family record says his brother "went abroad." You fill *Ambrose was ___* with the only word the reflection supports: *entombed*. CORRECT. A hairline crack appears under the eaves [u06]. It looks like a progress bar.

**The twist:** It is one. The Ushers' line had no "enduring branch" [u04] because one twin per generation was buried: Poe's "very temporary variation" was the vault. Your family tree and the fissure are the same zigzag. By the last generation the pattern is reflex: *Madeline was ___*, *entombed*. CORRECT: right about every detail, wrong about the woman. Something knocks under the ledger; the house splits and sinks [u50]. In the still tarn the reflection remains, every window lit, every buried twin looking up. All along, a hand moved in Madeline's reflected window. Players who noticed hold the word *living*: the game marks it INCORRECT, the crack stops, and a door opens.

**For everyone:** Spot the difference, then the chill: winning is the crime.

**For connoisseurs:** Poe's phrases are the answers, and knowing the tale makes you fill the last blank too fast.

**The 60-minute slice:** SVG facade with five windows and a vault, CSS-flipped into the tarn; five evidence cards (one drawn object, one line); a five-line click-to-fill ledger, checked in pairs; the fissure as a dash-offset path tied to the score; the split-and-sink; two endings.

**The risk:** If players never link the crack to their answers, the ending reads as spectacle, not guilt.
